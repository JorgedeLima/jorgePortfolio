# Research: how architects, builders and suppliers decide on products in New Zealand

Source: Jorge's desk research with ChatGPT, 3 October 2026. Shared chat: https://chatgpt.com/s/t_6ac04510e5ac819193ad3ae7482e2acc

This is a summary in our own words, with what it means for the prototype and case study. The legal and regulatory points come from the chat and have not been checked against the original sources yet. Check them before quoting any of them on the case study.

## The chain

Homeowner → architect → specification → builder (and quantity surveyor) → merchant or distributor → manufacturer → site.

Money, decisions and information travel in different directions along it:

- The **homeowner** describes outcomes and limits (needs, budget, timeframe, upkeep), not products. The first budget gap often appears here: what the client expects is not the same as the construction budget once fees, council costs, contingency and GST are counted.
- The **architect** turns the brief into a design (brief, concept, developed design, documentation, consent, tender, construction, completion) and products get more specific at each stage.
- The architect **chooses and specifies**. The **builder buys**. The **merchant supplies**. The **homeowner pays**. The person choosing is often not the person buying, so incentives are not fully aligned.
- Information also flows **backwards** from suppliers to architects: product data, samples, technical advice, pricing guidance, specification files, compliance documents, case studies.

## How architects pick products

- Discovery comes from past projects, supplier and manufacturer reps, other architects, builders, specification libraries (Masterspec is the main one in New Zealand), trade shows, publications, samples and platforms such as ArchiPro.
- Liking a product is not enough. Before specifying, an architect needs to know whether it meets the Building Code, how it is tested and documented, whether the council will accept its compliance path, how it works with the rest of the system, durability, warranty, installers, lead time, and whether replacements will exist in five years.
- Suppliers are therefore selling **evidence**, not just products. New Zealand requires set information for designated building products (scope and limits of use, relevant Building Code clauses, installation and maintenance), and there are formal assurance routes such as CodeMark.
- Being specified is commercially valuable: the architect buys nothing, but creates future demand.

## Where it goes wrong

- **Value engineering and substitution.** A builder can often get a cheaper alternative. It may be equivalent, or it may differ in durability, finish, warranty, installation, performance, upkeep or looks. If the original product was part of the consented design, a swap may need evidence that the new one meets the Building Code, and sometimes a consent amendment.
- **Price is hard to pin down.** Retail, the price an architect sees and the builder's trade price can all differ, and two builders can pay different prices for the same thing.
- **Odd cases:** a specified product is discontinued before tender; a client falls for an imported product that is unsuitable or has an 18-week lead time; a builder pushes their usual product; supplier relationships create familiarity bias; owner-supplied items arrive late or wrong; long-lead items must be ordered while the design is still moving; a product disappears mid-build; a look-alike substitute performs differently; the builder orders something slightly different without checking.

## What is particular to New Zealand

- A small market, so fewer suppliers can afford stock, technical teams, certification and installer networks. Imported products carry more supply risk.
- The Commerce Commission's market study found that the regulatory system has favoured familiar, "tried and tested" products, and that some supplier and merchant rebates make it harder for new products to enter. Familiar products are specified more, which makes them more familiar: a reinforcing loop.
- The Building Amendment Act 2025 added routes to make overseas products and standards easier to use (Building Product Specifications, Approved Products Certified Overseas, Endorsed Standards). The first Building Product Specifications took effect in July 2025.
- Work affecting primary structure or weathertightness may be Restricted Building Work, which needs licensed practitioners.

## The product insight

- ArchiPro looks like "Pinterest for building products", but the real journey runs from inspiration and discovery through shortlist, technical evaluation, compliance, specification, costing, availability, procurement, delivery and installation to warranty and maintenance. Different people own different stages.
- Each person asks a different question. Homeowner: "Do I like it?" Architect: "Can I responsibly specify it?" Builder: "Can I buy and install it reliably and profitably?" Supplier: "How do I get specified?" Council: "Can you show it complies?"
- The biggest friction is not discovery. It is **decision confidence**.
- A metric worth watching: discovered → shortlisted → specified → quoted → purchased, to see where value leaks out.
- Treat architect, homeowner, builder and supplier as four people in the same decision, not "architects are customers, suppliers are advertisers".

What already fits:

- The prototype closes the gap the research calls decision confidence: a shortlist becomes a reviewed, signed decision that both people can point to.
- The seeded Bathroom version 2 (a discontinued tile replaced, both people sign again) is one of the odd cases the research lists.
